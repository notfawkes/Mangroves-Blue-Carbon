A B S T R A C T
The mangroves conservation is essential due to the wide range of goods and ecosystem services. Despite their
importance, mangrove distribution has drastically decreased in recent decades, highlighting the need to
enhanced conservation efforts through innovative monitoring methods. Accordingly, this study presents an
approach that integrates remote sensing data in a study area with a diverse range of ecological scenarios,
comprising monospecific mangrove forests, which are dominated by a single species and mixed mangroves with
flooded freshwater forested wetlands. First, the integration of photogrammetric analysis from unmanned aerial
vehicles (UAVs) enables the precise determination of the spatial distribution of mangrove forests. Furthermore,
the multispectral data obtained from the Sentinel-2 satellite was employed for the training of multilayer perceptron
models (MLP) that are capable of accurately mapping mixed and monospecific mangrove forests. The
computational experiments for MLP models involve different number of neurons and hidden layers. The best
models for detecting mangrove forest achieved an accuracy of 99.95 % for training and 99.8 % for test, while for
monospecific mangrove forest, at the species level, it attained an accuracy of 98.73 % for training and 96.84 %
for test, in both cases the models demonstrated a great performance test for segmentation of different kind of
mangrove forests. By leveraging multi-source data and the capabilities of remote sensing and artificial intelligence
technologies, this approach offers a groundbreaking solution for monitoring mangrove ecosystems. Also,
this minimizes the economic and human efforts and reduces risk of error, thus leading to more efficient and
effective ecosystem conservation.
1. Introduction
Mangroves are evergreen forests that are found in tropical and subtropical
regions. The composition of mangrove forests can vary
considerably, contingent on the presence, abundance, diversity and
dominance of species. They may be classified as monospecific forests
when a single mangrove species is predominant, or as mixed forests
when multiple species are present, in conjunction with other wetland
vegetation. In general, they can tolerate high salt levels and grow on soft
substrates along the land-sea interface in intertidal environments such as
coastlines, shallow lagoons, estuaries, rivers, and deltas. These ecosystems
cover approximately 14.8 million hectares across 124 countries in
Asia, America, Africa, and Oceania. Recent estimation suggests the existence
of 50 to more than 70 mangrove species (FAO, 2007, 2023).
Mangroves provide a variety of ecosystem services, including regulating,
supporting, provisioning and cultural services. Regulating ecosystem
services maintain essential ecological processes and are the life support
system; supporting provide adequate space for animal and plant species

It is recognized that there has been a loss of mangrove forests
worldwide. Since the beginning of the 21st century, approximately 4.3
% of mangroves have been lost as a result of land use change for livestock,
aquaculture, urbanization and tourism development (Moreno
Casasola et al., 2002; Trouche et al., 2016). Consequently, numerous
studies have concentrated on monitoring changes in mangrove forests at
local, regional, and global scales since 1980 (CONABIO, 2016, 2021;
FAO, 2023; L´ opez-Portillo and Ezcurra, 2002; Rodríguez-Zu˜ niga et al.,

Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the United Nations Environment
Programme (UNEP) in collaboration with mangrove specialists worldwide,
have estimated 15.2 million hectares in 2005, compared to an
estimated total global mangrove area of 16.9 million hectares in 1990
(FAO, 2007). Various methods have been used to determine the distribution
of mangroves, such as aerial photography, field patrols, and
satellite sensors such as Sentinel-2, Landsat, Pl´ eiades, WorldView 2,
Spot, and Rapid Eye (Chopade et al., 2023; Rahmandhana et al., 2022;
Trouche et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018).
Remote sensing has enabled the monitoring of mangroves at various
scales by constantly gathering information from large areas and various
electromagnetic spectrum ranges. According to the United Nations Office
for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA), over 1000 earth observation
satellites were operational in 2023 with different spatial, spectral, and
temporal resolutions (Chuvieco, 2009; FAO, 2023; V´ azquez-Gonz´ alez
et al., 2017).
Although a significant proportion of this data is available at commercial
prices, there are few free options. In the case of Mexico, a
continuous monitoring program has been conducted every five years
since 2005, utilizing the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer
(MODIS) satellite system with a spatial resolution of 250 m to
identify changes in mangrove vegetation and estimation for biomass
(Rodríguez Zú˜ niga et al., 2018). Also, SPOT satellite imagery with a
resolution of 10 m in the visible and infrared range were employed for
the period from 2003 and stopped in 2007, due to the economic implications
of doing so (CONABIO, 2009; Vel´ azquez-Salazar et al., 2021).
This leads to considerable financial and human resource costs associated
with monitoring, coupled with the inconsistency of multispectral data
and spatial resolution when using different satellites that cannot be
compared. However, some satellites, such as Landsat and Sentinel-2,
provide free access and are often used in vegetation monitoring
studies. Landsat has been providing data since the 1980s with a 16-day
periodicity, 30-m spatial resolution and 10 bands at different wavelengths.
Sentinel-2, launched almost a decade ago, provides images with
a 10-day periodicity, a spatial resolution of up to 10 m and 12 bands at
different wavelengths (Chopade et al., 2023; Cissell et al., 2017; Rahmandhana
et al., 2022).
Neverthless, new technologies such as artificial intelligence can now
be used to improve monitoring methods (Avi˜ na-Hern´ andez et al., 2023;
Jhonnerie et al., 2015). The fusion of multi-source data, including
artificial intelligence and remote sensing technologies, can revolutionize
data analysis (Janga et al., 2023). Artificial intelligence in remote
sensing offers advantages such as improved data analysis capability,
data quality, and accuracy, in this case, for mangrove classification and
detection of cover changes. These methods have primarily been implemented
in countries such as China, Australia, India, Thailand, Indonesia,
Vietnam, among others (Janga et al., 2023; Pham et al., 2019; Vel´
azquez-Salazar
et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2020).
Although remote sensing data and artificial intelligence have been

studied extensively in other countries and methods have been shown to
be effective, there is a paucity of implementation in countries such as
Mexico, even though it is the fifth largest mangrove country in the world
(Zu-Ermagassen and Spalding, 2021). Moreover, although it has been
demonstrated that there are differences between species with respect to
their spectral responses (Rahmandhana et al., 2022; Valderrama-Landeros
et al., 2018), there is still a paucity of studies that explore the lack of
specificity to create models that can identify distribution at the species
level. This offers two potential avenues for further research: the opportunity
to create models for unstudied regions and for species-level
identification. Previous studies have investigated the potential of
various deep learning artificial intelligence models, including neural
networks, and have obtained excellent results (Ghorbanian et al., 2022;
Kong et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2008). Therefore, the present study took
the opportunity to implement the multilayer perceptron (MLP) model, a
neuronal network interconnected in a layered structure. This model
belongs to the category of machine learning and deep learning multiclass
models, due to its ability to solve a range of problems, including
those related to pixel-level classification and semantic segmentation,
through its simple or complex configuration. The model comprises three
layers: the input layer, which contains the data to be classified. Subsequently,
the hidden layer that receives the data from the previous layer,
which may be the input layer or another hidden layer. It then processes
and transforms the information. Finally, the output layer, which provides
the outcome of the network process(Russel and Norving, 2010).
Since the beginning of the century until the present, these models
have been used in some environmental issues that also use remote
sensing data (Gross et al., 1999; Keiner and Yan, 1998; Kimes et al.,
1996; Trujillo-Acatitla et al., 2022). In some countries, models for
monitoring mangroves have been developed using different methods of
artificial neuronal networks (ANN) using data from passive and active
sensors (Rahmandhana et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018; Zulfa et al.,
2021). Multilayer perceptron is one of the most widely used ANN
methods currently in use, as it is able to extract complex features that
help improve classification compared to traditional methods (Chollet,
2021; Goodfellow et al., 2017). The satellite data allows the identification
of unique patterns in pixel values across multiple spectral bands
representing different vegetation types (Chopade et al., 2023; Ghorbanian
et al., 2022). In the case of the present study, we sought to achieve a
more specific level, at the species level.
Although these methods have been implemented in some parts of the
world, further exploration is needed in Latin America and Mexico. It is
important to consider development at different scales, as the species
present in each region and their spectral responses differ. Integrating
technology, remote sensing, fieldwork, and artificial intelligence can
enhance ecological monitoring for preserving ecosystems such as mangroves.
This article presents an innovative methodology that integrates
unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) and satellite data with artificial intelligence
models to identify and monitor mangroves. Furthermore, this
methodology is expected to offer a more efficient and effective monitoring
solution, leading to reduced costs in terms of economic, human,
and time resources. Additionally, to the fact that this approach can be
extended to other types of vegetation, making it a valuable tool to
address the Sustainable Development Goals of the 2030 Agenda. Specifically,
it supports Goal 15, which aims to sustainably manage forests,
that entails the protection and restoration of terrestrial ecosystems, the
combating of desertification, the halting and reversal of land degradation,
and the halting of biodiversity loss.

2.1. Data acquisition
To achieve a more precise estimation of the distribution of mangroves,
field data of the actual distribution of each species was collected
in La Mancha, Veracruz and Arroyo Moreno, Veracruz Fig. 2. The site of
La Mancha (19◦35′ 12” N, 96◦23′ 09”W), Veracruz, Gulf of Mexico with
9050 km2 is a priority mangrove conservation site that has been a study
site for various researchers, producing maps and environmental data. It
was chosen as a pilot site due to the presence of well-defined monospecific
forests and easy accessibility to obtain control points for vegetation
verification for high-quality data.
A DJI Mavic Mini 2 UAV was used to conduct a zenithal drone shot of
the area at an altitude of 100 m in July 2021. A total of 5435 photos were
processed using the photogrammetry application Agisoft Metashape
Professional (Version 1.8.5) to obtain an orthophoto of the mangrove
forest area with the highest possible precision. Additionally, walking
and boat trips were conducted to verify the distribution of mangrove
species at 100 ground control points with a GPSMAP527. Later, polygons
of updated masks of monospecific of red (Rhizophora mangle), black
(Avicennia germinans) and white (Laguncularia racemosa) mangrove forest;
and mixed mangroves with flooded freshwater forested wetlands
were created using QGIS version 3.22.14, concerning the orthophoto
and field verification points.
Subsequently, a total of 147 cloud-free Sentinel-2 L1C image datasets
of the period 2015–2021 were obtained from the Coopernicus Browser
of the European Union’s Space programme and digital numbers were
converted into surface reflectance values. Also, the red-edge bands
(Band-5 0.705 μm, Band-6 0.740 μm, Band-7 0.783 μm and Band-8 A
0.865 μm) and shortwave infrared bands (Band-11 1.610 μm and Band-
12 2.190 μm) were resampling using the bilinear method with the rasterio
package in Python 3.8.13 to achieve a spatial resolution of 10 m,
such as the red, green, blue, and near-infrared bands.
The created polygons of the distribution of the mangrove forest were
used to extract the spectral information from the pixel values for each
band. The dataset was then normalized and standardized using the
standard scaler based on standard normally distributed data from Scikit
learn 1.2.1 in Python 3.8.13. Two class-balanced databases were created
using the TOA pixel values from Sentinel-2. The first one was a binary
database containing 60,000 pixel data according to what is mangrove,
where the mask of the three species were considered as one, and non-
mangrove; the second was a multiclass database containing 40,000
pixel data with four classes, created with each mask of the three species
(R. mangle, L. racemosa, and A. germinans) and non-mangrove areas. The
non-mangrove class was selected randomly from data belonging to
different classes including rainforest, crops, water, urban, dunes, and
other kind of wetlands.
2.2. Exploratory and statistical analysis
A simple variance analysis (One-Way ANOVA) was performed on
each band to evaluate whether there was a significant difference between
species. A Tukey’s post-hoc test was then conducted with a confidence
interval of 95 % (α of 0.05) to identify which species were
different. The bioinfokit v1 package in Python 3.8.13 was used for this
process (Kutner et al., 2004).
2.3. Multilayer perceptron for semantic segmentation
The multilayer perceptron is the best known and most widely used
type of neural network, whose architecture consists of multiple layers of
interconnected neurons that could be used for a sub-pixel classification.
It has non-linear activation functions, such as RELU, which allow it to
recognize complex patterns in data. It also has optimization algorithms,
such as ADAM, that improve model learning (Goodfellow et al., 2017).
In this study, the initial model consisted of a single input and one
output layer, with the sigmoid as the activation function, so that it could

the train test split module of scikit-learn 1.2.1, with 70 % for training
and 30 % for test.
For the binary models, the binary crossentropy was used as the loss
function and the sigmoid activation function in the output layer was
used, this one produces values ranging from 0 to 1, where 0 represents
the non-mangrove, and 1 represents mangrove. For the multiclass segmentation,
multiclass cross-entropy was used as the loss function, and
the softmax activation function in the output layer was used. The output
layer of the multiclass models produces a probability for each class; the
one with the highest probability is assigned to the corresponding class,
0 for non-mangrove, 1 for red mangrove (R. mangle), 2 for black
mangrove (A. germinans), 3 for white mangrove (L. racemosa).
Finally, a recent image of Sentinel-2 was used to assess the models’
performance test in La Mancha, where the acquisition data was taken
and from Arroyo Moreno (19◦ 00′ 59”N, 96◦ 03′
, 43”W), in Veracruz.

4. Discussion
Over time, several studies have used multispectral data from
different satellites for vegetation monitoring, including the development
of mangrove-specific indices such as Mangrove Vegetation Index (MVI)
and Combined Mangrove Recognition Index (CMRI) (Chen et al., 2023;
Roy et al., 2019; Tran et al., 2022). However, the results of the present
study have shown that by exploiting the potential of the technologies
together, given that there are spectral differences in the same bands
between species, multispectral data can be used directly to identify
mangrove distribution without the need for additional processing, such
as the use of vegetation indices.
Research on differences in spectral responses for mangroves has
mainly been conducted in countries such as China, India, and the United
States. However, there is a paucity of comprehensive studies on Latin
American species in this field (Arun Prasad and Gnanappazham, 2014;
Blasco et al., 1998; Rahmandhana et al., 2022; Zulfa et al., 2021). Avi˜
na-
Hern´ andez et al. (2023) is one of the few mangrove studies of Latin
America that employ artificial intelligence, utilizing drone images
within a spectral range of RGB and GRN (Green, Red and Near-infrared)
with a maximum accuracy of 0.77, in comparison to the present studies,
which exhibit a higher accuracy of over 0.90. Therefore, further research
is necessary in Latin America, an area with high biodiversity and
extensive mangrove areas, to better understand the region’s spectral
responses to mangroves and its potential for the development of monitoring
methods with artificial intelligence (Rahmandhana et al., 2022).
Even though the mangrove forest in Mexico covers 60 % of the coastline
there are few machine learning models developed for ecological applications
and limited information on the spectral features of mangrove

However, it is important to assess the suitability of artificial intelligence
models, focusing on species distribution at regional scales. In
other countries, models of artificial intelligence have been well-
developed, with species of mangroves distributed in different regions
using data from multiple sensors (Rahmandhana et al., 2022; Wang
et al., 2018; Xia et al., 2020). Nevertheless, the development of these
methodologies requires fundamental consideration of the conditions,
diversity and configuration of the ecosystems. According to some
studies, mangroves appearances and spectral features vary depending on
the region and species, making it challenging to generalize the models
(Aji et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2021).
It is possible that, given this situation, there are not many studies that
address the identification of vegetation at the species level. For this
reason, an advantage from the study area was taken since the presence of
monospecific mangrove forests of the three predominant species of
mangrove in Mexico. This offered an opportunity to obtain pure multispectral
data for each species and to deepen our understanding of their
specific spectral responses, in contrast to other locations where there is a
mingling of patches with different mangrove dominance and compositions.
In addition, many mangrove species are widely distributed
throughout the world. The red mangrove (R. mangle) is found in 34
countries, the black mangrove (A. germinans) in 38 countries and the
white mangrove (L. racemosa) in 33 countries. Their distribution covers
the continents of America and Africa, as well as island areas (Plants of

This was used as an opportunity to develop two distinct models with
the objective of creating a multi-purpose tool. The first model differentiates
between mangrove and non-mangrove areas, regardless of the
species, in order to be employed in ecosystems where mangroves co-
occur with other vegetation. The second model was focused on monospecific
forest mangrove to facilitate conservation decision-making or
regeneration needs for specific species. In both cases, the models are
applicable to Mexico and different regions of the world, where there are
only a few studies employing similar methodologies (Avi˜ na-Hern´ andez
et al., 2023; Cissell et al., 2017). Although studies in countries such as
Iran and Vietnam have developed similar methods for mangrove identification
(Ghorbanian et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2020; Zu-Ermagassen and
Spalding, 2021), these have not been developed at the species level and
cannot be generalized to other countries with different species
compositions.
The results demonstrated differences in performance between binary
and multi-class models, which can be attributed to the inherent variability
of the multispectral response, as previously pointed out by
several authors (Arun Prasad and Gnanappazham, 2014; Zulfa et al.,
2021). Also, the results indicated the necessity of assessing the efficacy
of the models, as the highest accuracies did not necessarily yield the
most accurate spatial distributions when testing the models. As

mangrove (L. racemosa) in this area, with the presence of flooded
freshwater forested wetlands (L´ opez-Portillo et al., 2002). Others report

of mainly black (A. germinans) and white (L. racemosa) mangrove
(Environmental-Consultancy, 2022; Hern´ andez-Trejo et al., 2006). Red
mangrove (R. mangle) dominance is also reported in some permanently
flooded areas in the region (Environmental-Consultancy, 2022). This is
consistent with the models and demonstrates the potential of multi-class
models to identify mangrove species dominance, despite having mixed
pixels with other vegetation types.
In the multiclass models, the variability of the data increases, which
makes them more sensitive in comparison to the binary models. In this
sense, it is important to highlight that MLP model approximations can
vary according to their architecture, and the comparison between the
results in Figs. 8 and 9 shows that a model with fewer neurons can offer
significant advantages in terms of simplicity, generalisation and effectiveness
in finding relevant patterns with more efficient performance in
identifying monospecific forests, thus avoiding overfitting (Hagan et al.,
2014). This is therefore a demonstration of the potential of multi-class
artificial intelligence models for the monitoring of species-specific distribution
changes.
With regard to the artificial intelligence methods employed, several
studies have shown that ANN can achieve similar accuracies using algorithms
such as RetinaNet (Chen et al., 2023; Ghorbanian et al., 2022)
when using multispectral data from Sentinel-2. Other research indicates
that incorporating multiple indices in the input features can significantly
enhance and improve the models. However, this study only employed
the original multispectral data and obtained better accuracies, which
indicates that obtaining the data in its purest form for model training is
preferable, rather than relying on indices (Guo et al., 2021; Maung and
Sasaki, 2021).

Biodiversity (CONABIO) in Mexico has implemented a mangrove
monitoring program on a five-year cycle, a robust monitoring method
for mangroves has not yet been fully developed. To date satellite images
have been employed with a spatial resolution of 250 m, such as MODIS,
others with a resolution of 30 m, such as Landsat, and with a resolution

offers a reduction in the human effort required for field sampling, as well
as the potential to reduce the human risks from fieldwork to determine
changes after a natural disaster. Also, this is coupled with the opportunity
to be extrapolated to any part of the world by integrating enough
species-specific data.
4.1. Considerations and future research
Previously, mangrove monitoring was conducted through field surveys,
light aircraft or helicopter flights, and the use of costly satellite
imagery. However, technological advances have enabled the use of
drones for high-spatial-resolution mapping and open-access satellites,
such as Sentinel-2, for the acquisition of a vast quantity of multispectral
data. This has the dual benefit of reducing economic costs, human effort
and improving data quality. Furthermore, artificial intelligence models
have demonstrated considerable potential for ecological applications in
the monitoring of ecosystems such as mangroves.
However, several factors warrant consideration. It has been
demonstrated that there is variation in reflectance between species.
Consequently, the pixel information in a satellite image may comprise a
mixed composition of reflectances. It is therefore essential to consider
this factor when interpreting the results of artificial intelligence models.
This manifested as an erroneous classification of the red mangrove
(R. mangle) as water in some multi-class models.
Similarly, it is crucial to consider the composition of the ecosystems
and the degree of generalisation inherent to the models. In the present
study, mixed mangrove areas were considered; however, it should be
noted that mangroves can vary in their reflectance depending on the
presence, diversity and dominance of species. The present study has
demonstrated the potential for mapping at a more specific level with
these technologies. However, as knowledge of this is further developed
and integrated, the method can be improved to other ecosystems and

5. Conclusions
The acquisition of data from multiple sources confers several advantages,
which expand the possibilities beyond traditional monitoring
methods, which are typically carried out using in situ measurements in
the field to verify the ground truth, which incurs in high economic,
human, and time costs. The use of UAVs offers the potential to obtain
information on the distribution of mangroves in areas that are difficult
to access. Conversely, multispectral data from optical satellites, such as
Sentinel-2, is freely available and provides consistent information over
time. Finally, the application of artificial intelligence algorithms as a
novel tool has proven useful for differentiation vegetation down to the
species level, which opens up new possibilities for mangrove monitoring,
where the development and implementation of these new technologies
is needed.
This methodology may be employed as an alternative to monitoring
monospecific and mixed mangrove forests, thereby affording the opportunity
to identify areas of ecological vulnerability based on the
condition of the mangroves. The objective of this study is to facilitate the
monitoring of mangroves in Mexico and different regions of the world,
thereby human risks from fieldwork through the implementation of
remote monitoring. Furthermore, it is a cost-effective solution with high
efficacy and efficiency, which will facilitate the implementation of long-
term government programs aimed at achieving objectives such as
mangrove mapping, conservation, and the enhancement of regeneration
efficiency in the context of climate change and coastal vulnerability.
